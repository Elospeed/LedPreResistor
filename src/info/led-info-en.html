Basics

How the light is made

An LED (light emitting diode) is a semiconductor diode operated in the forward direction. With plus on the anode and minus on the cathode, electrons from the N layer and holes from the P layer move into the junction. There they recombine and release energy as light.

The colour depends on the semiconductor material, more precisely on its band gap Eg. The larger the band gap, the shorter the wavelength and the higher the forward voltage. That is why a blue LED needs about 3 V and a red one only about 2 V.

λ [nm] ≈ 1240 / Eg [eV]Wavelength from the band gap. Example: Eg = 2.0 eV gives about 620 nm, i.e. red.
UF ≳ Eg / eThe forward voltage is roughly the band gap in volts, plus losses in the chip.

Two material systems dominate today: AlInGaP for red, orange and yellow, InGaN for UV, blue, green and (with phosphor) white. Infrared LEDs (IREDs) are based on GaAs or AlGaAs.

Important: An LED has almost no resistance of its own. Above the forward voltage the current rises very steeply. Without current limiting (series resistor or constant current source) it burns out within seconds.

Between green and yellow (about 540–590 nm) LEDs are still much less efficient than blue or red ones. This gap is called the “green gap”. When in doubt, the datasheet of the actual LED always applies.

UV and IR: Both kinds of radiation are barely or not at all visible to the eye, but can damage it. Never look straight into them, even if “nothing is lit”. IR LEDs can be checked with a phone camera.

White light

How a white LED is made

Blue plus phosphor

Almost all white LEDs today are blue InGaN LEDs with a phosphor layer (usually YAG:Ce). The phosphor converts part of the blue light into yellow and red. Together this gives white. The amount and type of phosphor set the shade.

RGB mixing

Alternatively, a red, a green and a blue chip sit in the same package. Driving them separately mixes any colour. Addressable RGB LEDs (e.g. WS2812) have the driver built in and are controlled over a data line; they need no series resistor.

Colour temperature and colour rendering

2700–3000 K warm white, 4000 K neutral white, 5000–6500 K cool white. The colour rendering index CRI (Ra) tells how natural colours look: 80 and up is good, 90 and up very good.

Connection

Telling the polarity

An LED only lights in the forward direction. Connected the wrong way round, usually nothing happens as long as the reverse voltage (typically only 5 V) is not exceeded. To remember: cathode = short leg = flat edge.

A multimeter in diode test mode tells for sure: if the LED glows faintly or the meter shows a voltage, the red probe is on the anode. The larger electrode inside is often, but not always, the cathode.

AC voltage: Because the reverse voltage is so low, an LED on AC needs protection: a diode (e.g. 1N4148) in anti-parallel to the LED or in series, or two LEDs in anti-parallel. On mains voltage (230 V) no home-made solutions; use ready-made, approved drivers.

Calculation

Calculating the series resistor

The series resistor takes the voltage the LED does not need and so sets the current. The calculation is simple. What matters is to round up to the next standard value afterwards and to check the resistor’s power.

UR = U − n · UFvoltage across the resistor (n = number of LEDs in series)
R = UR / IFseries resistor in Ω, current in A
I = UR / RE12actual current with the standard value
PR = UR · Ipower dissipated in the resistor
Ptot = U · Ipower of the whole circuit
η = n · UF / Ushare that reaches the LED

Which current?

Standard LEDs (3 mm, 5 mm) are usually rated for at most 20 mA. Modern LEDs are so efficient, though, that 2–10 mA is often enough for an indicator. Less current means less heat, longer life and longer battery runtime.

How many watts?

For continuous use a resistor should be loaded with at most half of its rated power. That is why LedPreResistor warns from 50% load. As a guide, a ¼ W resistor heats up by about 200–250 °C per watt above ambient. LedPreResistor uses 225 °C/W, larger sizes correspondingly less (½ W about half).

    Circuits

    Several LEDs

    In series: the better choice

    In a series circuit the same current flows through all LEDs. The forward voltages add up and one shared series resistor is enough. Leave at least 1–2 V of headroom across the resistor, otherwise the current varies a lot when the supply or the temperature changes.

    12 V: 3 × white (9.6 V) + 2.4 V across the resistor

    Parallel: each LED with its own resistor

    Even LEDs from the same batch have slightly different forward voltages. On top of that, UF drops as the temperature rises (about −2 mV/K). If several LEDs share one resistor, the “weakest” one draws the most current, gets warmer, draws even more and fails first.

    Alternatives to the resistor

    Constant current and dimming

    When constant current?

    A series resistor is ideal for a fixed voltage and small currents. If the supply varies (battery, car with 12–14.4 V) or it is about power LEDs from around 350 mA, a constant current source is better.

    A classic: the LM317 as a current source with R = 1.25 V / I. For 20 mA that gives 62 Ω. It needs about 3 V of headroom and works reliably from around 10 mA.

    Power LEDs

    For 1 W and 3 W LEDs and high-power LED strips, use ready-made switching drivers (buck drivers). They reach over 90% efficiency, while a resistor simply turns the voltage difference into heat. Power LEDs always need a heat sink.

    Dimming with PWM

    LEDs are best dimmed with pulse width modulation: the LED is switched on and off very quickly and its colour stays the same. To avoid visible flicker (also on cameras), the frequency should be at least 1 kHz.

    In a car: The on-board voltage is up to 14.4 V while charging. Calculate the series resistor with this value, otherwise too much current flows with the engine running.

    Operation

    Heat and lifetime

    LEDs do not age from a wearing filament but from heat. The hotter the junction, the faster the brightness drops. Manufacturers usually state the lifetime as L70: the time until the LED still has 70% of its original brightness. 25,000–50,000 hours are typical.

    Modern white LEDs reach 150–220 lm/W, over 300 lm/W in the lab. For comparison: an incandescent bulb manages about 12 lm/W, a fluorescent tube 60–100 lm/W. Still, a large part of the power becomes heat that has to be removed.

    Static electricity: Blue, white, green and UV LEDs (InGaN) are sensitive to electrostatic discharge. Touch a grounded object briefly before handling them.

    History

    How the LED developed

    Since the 1960s the light output per LED has grown by a factor of about 20 every ten years, while the price per lumen fell to a tenth. This rule of thumb is called Haitz's law.

    1. 1962Nick Holonyak builds the first LED with visible, red light (GaAsP). Efficiency about 0.1 lm/W.
    2. 1972George Craford develops yellow LEDs and makes red LEDs much brighter.
    3. 1990sAlInGaP brings bright red, orange and yellow LEDs that are good enough for traffic lights and tail lights for the first time.
    4. 1993Shuji Nakamura presents the first bright blue LED made of InGaN.
    5. 1996First white LED: blue chip with yellow phosphor.
    6. 2014Nobel Prize in Physics to Akasaki, Amano and Nakamura for the blue LED.
    7. todayWhite LEDs with 150–220 lm/W are standard. Incandescent bulbs are largely banned in the EU and Switzerland.
    For the workbench

    Practical tips

    • Never connect an LED directly to a voltage source. Always use a series resistor or a constant current source.
    • Round up the series resistor to the next E12 value. Rounding down increases the current.
    • Check the power dissipation and load the resistor to at most 50%.
    • Test with a low current (5–10 mA) first. Often that is bright enough.
    • Look up UF and the maximum current in the datasheet; the table above only gives guide values.
    • Solder quickly and not too hot (max. about 3 s at 260 °C), and do not bend the legs right at the package.
    • LEDs in parallel each get their own resistor.
    • For battery or car use, calculate with the highest possible voltage.
